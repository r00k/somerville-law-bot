Somerville's rules on where pumped or draining water may go live in three places: the stormwater/sewer provisions (Sec. 11-165), the illicit-discharge division (Sec. 11-176), and — easy to miss because it sits in the streets-and-sidewalks chapter — Sec. 12-2 on water run-off.

**Where the water is allowed to go.** Stormwater may only discharge to the Municipal Separate Storm Sewer System (MS4), and must be kept out of combined sewers, sanitary sewers, and storm drains that discharge to combined sewers (Sec. 11-165(a)). Sump-pump water and water from exterior foundation drains, footing drains, and crawl-space pumps are expressly exempt from the ban on nonstormwater discharges to the MS4 — provided the source is not a significant contributor of a pollutant, and sewage-contaminated water is never allowed (Sec. 11-176(g)(2)–(4)). Physically connecting a private drain into a main drain or common sewer requires a permit from the city engineer, who must be satisfied the connection won't carry roof water or surface water into the sanitary system (Sec. 11-165(d)).

**Where it is not allowed to go — streets and sidewalks.** Sec. 12-2 is the on-point rule for water flowing over the public way, and it covers more than car washing: no person "using water … for other purposes" may use it or allow it to escape "as to flow or collect upon any street, or to flow upon or across the surface of any sidewalk" (Sec. 12-2(b)), and no building or projection may shed water onto a sidewalk or street to the injury or inconvenience of travelers (Sec. 12-2(a)). Letting a sump pump outlet sheet across the sidewalk into the gutter runs into this section.

**Neighboring lots.** No impervious surface may be constructed, expanded, or altered so that it increases stormwater runoff onto adjacent lots or any right-of-way (Sec. 11-145). This is a construction-triggered rule — it doesn't say where pumped water may be pointed — but it is the code's clearest statement that pushing more runoff onto a neighbor is not an option. (The swimming-pool exemption in Sec. 11-176(g)(7) reflects the same principle: draining may not "flood or otherwise adversely affect neighboring or downstream properties.")

**Enforcement and authority.** The city engineer administers and enforces the illicit-discharge division and can adopt regulations (Sec. 11-176(d)–(e)), and can order a property's drainage changed — and even cut off noncompliant private drains — with the mayor's approval (Sec. 11-165(b)–(c)).

**Practical picture for a small lot.** Keeping the discharge on your own lot to infiltrate (e.g., a drywell or splash area away from the sidewalk and lot lines), or getting a permitted tie-in to the storm drain through the city engineer, are the routes the code contemplates. The code does not set a required setback, pipe size, or discharge method for sump pumps; those details would come from the engineering department. State plumbing and building codes (outside this corpus) also bear on how foundation drainage is constructed.
